# Neighbourhood operations

Exposure, curves and blend modes compute each output pixel from the same pixel below. Blur, sharpen and clarity read a neighbourhood around it. That one difference sets their cost, decides how they fit into tiles, and is the reason the engine renders a tile's backdrop larger than the tile. This chapter derives the Gaussian blur as three box blurs, builds sharpening from it, and measures how far each operation reaches.

## 4.1 Convolution

A **convolution** replaces each pixel with a weighted sum of its neighbours, using the same weights, the **kernel**, everywhere. A kernel of radius <i>r</i> in two dimensions has <i>(2r + 1)²</i> weights, so a direct blur of radius 16 reads 1,089 pixels per output pixel. The Gaussian kernel is **separable**: its 2D weights are the product of a 1D kernel along x and the same along y. Blurring the rows and then the columns gives the same result for <i>2(2r + 1)</i> reads, 66 at radius 16.

## 4.2 Box blurs and the running sum

A **box blur** gives equal weight to the <i>w = 2r + 1</i> pixels in a window. Its cost does not depend on <i>w</i> at all: slide the window one pixel along and the sum gains the pixel entering on the right and loses the one leaving on the left. The engine's horizontal pass does this for four channels at once:

`k` indexes the pixel entering the window and `q` the one leaving it; four running sums `a0` to `a3` are updated with one addition and one subtraction each, and multiplied by `norm`, which is <i>1 / w</i>. The loops before and after this one handle the ends of the row, where the window would read outside it; they repeat the first or last pixel instead.

Gaussian blurs are specified by their **standard deviation** <i>σ</i>, and variances add under convolution. A box of width <i>w</i> has variance <i>(w² − 1) / 12</i>, so three boxes of widths <i>w₁, w₂, w₃</i> make a Gaussian-like blur with

```math
σ² = (w₁² + w₂² + w₃² − 3) / 12
```

Three boxes of width 5 give <i>σ² = 3 × 2 = 6</i>. The engine picks the widths from <i>σ</i> the other way round, choosing two odd widths that differ by 2 and how many of each:

```js

```

For <i>σ</i> = 6 px it returns 11, 11 and 13; for <i>σ</i> = 24 px, 47, 47 and 49.

## 4.4 Sharpen and clarity

**Unsharp masking** sharpens by adding back the detail a blur removes: <i>C' = C + a (C − blur(C))</i>, where <i>a</i> is the amount. Where the picture is flat, <i>C − blur(C)</i> is zero and nothing changes; at an edge it is positive on the light side and negative on the dark side, so the edge steepens. A small radius (1 to 2 px) sharpens fine texture. **Clarity** is the same formula at a large radius, around 24 px, with the amount multiplied by <i>1 − (2L − 1)²</i>, where <i>L</i> is the pixel's luminance. That weight is 1 at mid-grey and 0 at black and white, so local contrast rises in the midtones without clipping the ends.

## 4.5 Reach: what a tile must read

A pointwise layer can render one tile from the same tile below it. A blur cannot: an output pixel depends on input pixels up to the sum of the three box radii away. The engine calls this the **reach**:

```js
// engine.js · blurReach
{{src:blurReach}}
```

To render a tile under a blur, the engine first assembles the tile below plus a border of the reach on every side (an **apron**), blurs that, and keeps the middle. For <i>σ</i> = 6 px the reach is 16 px; at <i>σ</i> = 24 px it is 70 px. The apron is work done again for every neighbouring tile, and [Chapter 5](lab:book?ch=5) measures what that costs.

## 4.6 Key points

- A convolution weights a neighbourhood; separable kernels cost a row pass plus a column pass.
- A box blur costs the same at any width, thanks to a running sum.
- Three box blurs approximate a Gaussian; their widths come from adding variances.
- Sharpen and clarity add back <i>C − blur(C)</i>, at a small radius or a large one weighted to the midtones.
- An operation that reads neighbours forces each tile to read an apron as wide as its reach.
