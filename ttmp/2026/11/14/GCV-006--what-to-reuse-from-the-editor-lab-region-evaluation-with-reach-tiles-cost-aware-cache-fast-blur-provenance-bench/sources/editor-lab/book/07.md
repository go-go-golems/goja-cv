# Scheduling and measuring

Pyramids, tiles and caches make work cheaper; a scheduler decides when to do it, and in what order, and when to give up on it. This chapter starts from the frame budget and where D-01's time goes, describes the stage's renderer, which shows a coarse frame at once and refines in slices, and then turns to what a production engine adds: priorities, cancellation and workers. Along the way it ports GIMP's adaptive chunk-size rule and reads darktable's cancellation rule and the browser's scheduling APIs against the lab's renderer. The second half is about measuring: percentiles, the clock the page is given, the trap of measuring service time instead of response time, noise, and modelled devices. By the end you will be able to size a unit of work from the frame budget, judge an adaptive step rule by simulating it, decide when to cancel, and measure latency without coordinated omission.

## 7.1 The frame budget, and where the time goes

At 60 Hz the display shows a new frame every 16.7 ms, and everything the page does between two frames (input, layout, the render, drawing the stage) has to fit in that time or a frame is skipped. At 120 Hz the budget is 8.3 ms.

A full cold render of D-01 takes about 280 ms on the machine that built this book (Table 5.2), seventeen frames. Table 6.1 splits it by layer: the other seven layers cost 17 to 33 ms each, Sharpen 87 ms. The render cannot fit in a frame, so the renderer does not try. It aims for two things instead: show something correct within the frame of the change, and the full answer as soon as possible after it, without ever blocking input for long.

The second aim sets a limit on the unit of work. JavaScript on the main thread cannot be interrupted, so the longest single step decides how long input can wait. For a full stack, one tile costs about 2.2 ms at 64 px, 7.1 ms at 128 px and 24 ms at 256 px (Table 5.2 divided by the tile counts). A 256 px tile is longer than a frame on its own: with large tiles, the renderer must either split a tile's layers across frames or move the work off the main thread (Section 7.6).

When the document changes, the renderer draws a **preview** at a coarse pyramid level ([Chapter 5](lab:book?ch=5)) in the same frame, then refines at level 0 tile by tile, at most 8 ms of tiles per frame, visible tiles first and nearest the middle of the view first:

While a gesture lasts (a slider drag, a brush stroke) the renderer keeps to previews and starts refining 220 ms after the last change. Whether to show a preview at all depends on an estimate of the full render's cost, a moving average of the last few: during a gesture a preview is drawn when the full render is expected to take more than 10 ms, otherwise when it is expected to take more than 40 ms. In Auto, the preview's level follows its own cost: if a preview took more than 12 ms the next one is a level coarser, down to level 3, and if it took under 2.5 ms a level finer, up to level 1.

Each pass of the `while` takes one tile from the queue, asks the engine for it (from the cache or computed), and draws it into the display canvas. The loop stops when the queue is empty or the slice is spent, unless the policy is Off or the full render is expected to take under 12 ms, in which case it runs to the end in one go. A tile whose computation count went up is recorded so the tile overlay can outline it in red.

A new edit during refinement empties the queue and starts again from a new preview. Tiles finished for the old state are not wasted: their keys are in the cache, and if the edit did not touch them they are hits next time. This is **cancellation by generation**: work is abandoned between tiles, never inside one.

What the preview buys is large. On D-01 at 128 px tiles, scrubbing the lowest adjustment has a median update of 259 ms with previews off and 12.7 ms with them on; the 95th percentile goes from 287 ms to 28 ms. The price is the **settle** time, 287 ms of refinement after the gesture ends, during which the stage shows the preview and then fills in.

## 7.3 Adapting the size of a step

The lab's renderer fixes the size of its step, one tile, and checks the clock between steps. GIMP's projection, the part that composites the layer stack for display, does the reverse: it renders the image in chunks whose size it adapts so that each chunk takes a fixed time. The rule is in `app/core/gimpchunkiterator.c`, and it is short.

1. The first target area is one GEGL tile, 128 × 128 = 16,384 pixels.
2. After a chunk of area <i>a</i> that took Δ<i>t</i>, the aim is <i>a</i> · <i>I</i> / Δ<i>t</i>: the area that would have taken exactly one interval <i>I</i>, 1/15 s by default.
3. The target is the median of the last three aims, so one noisy measurement cannot move it.
4. Chunks smaller than 4,096 pixels do not update the target, so slivers at the edge of a region do not distort it.
5. An iteration stops once it has used more than <i>I</i>; the priority region, the visible part of the canvas, is rendered first; and updates are aligned to 32 × 32 px chunks.

How does the rule behave when the work changes under it? Figure 7.1 runs a port of steps 1 to 4 against a cost model, 0.2 ms per chunk plus 30 ns per pixel, with the cost per pixel four times higher for chunks 10 to 19, as when an expensive layer is switched on and later off.

**Figure 7.1.** Time per chunk under GIMP's chunk-size rule, computed by a port of the rule (`tools/figs2.py`, and the program below) against the stated cost model; not a measurement of GIMP. The dashed line is the 1/15 s interval the rule aims at. Look at chunks 10 and 11: their size was chosen for the old cost, so each takes four times the interval, 266 ms, before the median catches up; at chunks 20 and 21 the reverse happens, and two chunks of 17 ms leave most of the interval unused.

Three things follow from the trace.

- **Fixed costs vanish.** The steady chunk is 2.2 megapixels, and its 0.2 ms of overhead is 0.3 % of the interval. A rule that adapts the step can afford tiny per-step overheads to be paid rarely, which a fixed 64 px tile cannot.
- **It learns from the past, so it overshoots after a change.** The overshoot is the ratio of new cost to old, here four, and the median, which protects against noise, makes it overshoot twice instead of once. The lab's renderer has the opposite failure and the opposite bound: its step never adapts, but it never overshoots by more than one tile, 7 ms at 128 px.
- **The interval decides the latency.** One fifteenth of a second is four frames at 60 Hz. An engine that must answer input in every frame on the same thread needs an interval under a frame, or the work must move off that thread (Section 7.6).

A tiled engine cannot adapt the tile itself, because the tile is also the cache's unit, but it can adapt how many tiles it takes per slice, which the lab does implicitly by checking the clock. It can also do better than learning from the past: the graph compiler of [Chapter 9](lab:book?ch=9) knows which layers a tile passes through and can estimate the cost before it starts, so switching on an expensive layer changes the estimate before the first slow tile.

```run gimp-chunks.js
// A port of GIMP's chunk-size rule (app/core/gimpchunkiterator.c) run against a cost model:
// 0.2 ms a chunk plus 30 ns a pixel, four times that per pixel for chunks 10 to 19. Change it and rerun.
const INTERVAL = 1000 / 15, MIN_AREA = 4096, HISTORY = 3;
const cost = (k, area) => 0.2 + area * (k >= 10 && k < 20 ? 120e-6 : 30e-6);   // ms
let target = 128 * 128, hist = []; const rows = [];
for (let k = 0; k < 30; k++) {
  const area = Math.round(target), ms = cost(k, area);
  rows.push({ chunk: k, pixels: area, ms: +ms.toFixed(1), late: ms > 1.5 * INTERVAL ? 'late' : '' });
  if (area >= MIN_AREA) {                       // GIMP's update: aim for one chunk per interval,
    hist = [...hist, area * INTERVAL / ms].slice(-HISTORY);              // and take the median
    target = [...hist].sort((a, b) => a - b)[hist.length >> 1];           // of the last three aims
  }
}
return rows;
```

> **Exercise.** Change the program so that the target is the newest aim alone, without the median. How many chunks overshoot after the step now? Then add random noise of ±30 % to every chunk's cost and compare the two rules by the number of chunks over 1.5 intervals.

## 7.4 Priorities

The renderer above has two priorities, preview and refinement. A production engine needs more, because there is always more work than time. In order of urgency:

1. **Input and overlays.** Pointer handling, handles, the cursor and the selection outline must respond every frame, whatever else is running.
2. **The gesture's preview.** One coarse render per input event, coalesced so that only the newest event is rendered.
3. **Visible tiles at the zoom's level**, centre first, because people look at the middle.
4. **Neighbours of the view**, so that a pan shows finished tiles at its edge.
5. **Everything else**: other pyramid levels, thumbnails in the layers panel, histograms, the composite for export.
6. **Persistence**: writing tiles or the document to disk.

Within the main thread the browser offers few ways to express this. `requestAnimationFrame` runs before each frame; a task posted to a `MessageChannel` runs soon after the current one; `requestIdleCallback` runs work when the browser expects to be idle. The Prioritized Task Scheduling API adds `scheduler.postTask`, which takes one of three priorities, `user-blocking`, `user-visible` and `background`, and `scheduler.yield()`, which ends the current task and continues the function in a new one. A continuation after `scheduler.yield()` is scheduled ahead of other queued tasks of the same priority, so a long render that yields between tiles does not go to the back of the queue. `scheduler.yield()` is in Chrome and Edge from version 129 and Firefox from 142, and not in Safari, so an engine should detect each API and fall back to a `MessageChannel` post or `setTimeout`, accepting that the fallback loses the priority. The firmer tool is to keep levels 3 to 6 off the main thread altogether.

**Coalescing** belongs with priorities. A slider drag produces pointer events faster than a slow engine can render them. Rendering each in turn builds a queue and makes the delay grow with the length of the drag; rendering only the newest, and dropping the ones it supersedes, keeps the delay to at most two updates. The stage's `request` does this by setting a flag, not by queuing work.

## 7.5 Cancellation

Abandoning work is cheap only if it was cut into small enough pieces. The renderer checks between tiles, so the worst delay before it notices a new edit is one tile, 7 ms at 128 px. Three refinements matter in a larger engine.

- **Generations.** Each document state has a number. A job records the generation it was started for; before starting, and before delivering, it checks whether that generation is still current.
- **Keep what finished.** With content keys a finished tile is correct for its key forever, so a result that arrives late goes into the cache even though nothing is waiting for it. An engine with invalidation would have to throw it away.
- **Cancel inside long tiles.** A tile that takes longer than a frame (the 24 ms of a 256 px tile, or a large blur) should be cancellable between layers, or between the row and column passes of a blur. Each check is one comparison; the cost is in structuring the code so that a layer can stop and resume.

Production engines make the same decisions with different rules.

- **darktable** decides by age. When a parameter changes while the full pipe is running, it compares the time since that run started with the pipe's average run time. If less than half the average has passed, it neither stops the run nor queues another: the run finishes, and because the history changed meanwhile, the job then restarts the pipe with the new parameters. If more than half has passed, it sets the pipe's atomic shutdown flag, which the pipe checks between modules, and the run stops and restarts. Its source gives the reason as fewer superfluous runs and less pressure on the cache. In effect, changes that arrive during the first half of a run are coalesced into one restart after it.
- **Chromium's compositor** cannot cancel a raster task once it is running. It cancels the tasks that have not started by submitting a new task graph that leaves them out, so its unit of cancellation is one tile's raster, as the lab's is one tile.
- **Krita** keeps two strokes for one gesture under Instant Preview ([Chapter 5](lab:book?ch=5)): the coarse one the person sees and the full one that is computed after it, from the beginning.

> **Careful.** darktable's rule needs an average run time, and the average depends on what the cache already holds: a pipe that finds most modules cached finishes in a fraction of its cold time. An engine that copies the rule should keep the average per kind of change, or the rule will cancel too eagerly after cold runs and too late after warm ones.

## 7.6 Workers, OffscreenCanvas and shared memory

A **Web Worker** runs JavaScript on another thread, with its own event loop and no access to the page. Moving tile computation to a pool of workers removes the frame budget from the work itself: the main thread only handles input, draws the overlays and puts finished tiles on screen. A pool of one worker per core, minus one for the main thread, is the usual size; `navigator.hardwareConcurrency` reports the number of logical cores, 2 in the container that built this book.

Workers communicate by messages, and the cost is in the data. An `ArrayBuffer` can be **transferred**: ownership moves to the receiver without copying, and the sender's view becomes empty. A 128 px float tile (256 KiB) moves in constant time this way, where a structured copy costs time in proportion to its size. A `SharedArrayBuffer` can be seen by several threads at once, which lets workers share one tile cache and the source rasters; it is available only when the page is **cross-origin isolated**, which a server arranges with two response headers, `Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy: same-origin` and `Cross-Origin-Embedder-Policy: require-corp` (or `credentialless`). The Bench's WASM block reports whether this page is isolated; as a published artifact it is not, so threads and shared memory are off here and a worker design would have to transfer tiles.

An **OffscreenCanvas** is a canvas a worker can draw into. A canvas element on the page can hand its drawing surface to a worker with `transferControlToOffscreen()`, after which a worker can composite tiles and present them without the main thread. With WebGPU the same applies to a GPU canvas context ([Chapter 8](lab:book?ch=8)).

Isolation changes one more thing: timer precision. Browsers coarsen `performance.now()` to make timing attacks harder, and coarsen it more on pages that are not isolated. On this page, in the container that built this book, the smallest step between two readings was 0.1 ms. That is the rule Chrome adopted in version 91, and the High Resolution Time specification states it: 100 µs on pages that are not cross-origin isolated, 5 µs on pages that are. WebGPU's timestamp queries are coarsened to 100 µs in Chrome for the same reason ([Chapter 8](lab:book?ch=8)). A 0.1 ms clock is fine for timing a tile and too coarse for timing a single small kernel; repeat the kernel and divide. The program below finds the step on your browser.

```run timer.js
// The clock this page gets: the smallest step between two different performance.now() readings,
// and whether the page is cross-origin isolated (5 µs steps) or not (100 µs).
let min = Infinity; const seen = new Set();
for (let i = 0; i < 2000; i++) {
  const a = performance.now(); let b; do b = performance.now(); while (b === a);
  min = Math.min(min, b - a); if (seen.size < 6) seen.add(+(b - a).toFixed(4));
}
return { crossOriginIsolated, smallest_step_ms: +min.toFixed(4), some_steps_ms: [...seen] };
```

Threads in other engines show what a pool can do when the work is shaped for it. libvips gives each thread a cheap copy of the writeable state of the whole pipeline, so its threads need only four lock operations per output tile, however long the pipeline. Photoshop on the web depends on threads, and bringing dynamic multithreading to WebAssembly was one of the requirements for the port; Emscripten maps its pthreads onto workers sharing one memory, which needs the cross-origin isolation described above.

## 7.7 What to measure

The number that matters to the person dragging a slider is **update latency**: from a change to the next frame that shows it. The bench reports it per update, for every update of a scenario, and summarises the list by its **percentiles**. The median, p50, is the typical update; p95 is the update you notice, the one in twenty that is slow. An average would hide it: fifty-seven updates of 5 ms and three of 200 ms average 14.8 ms, under budget, while the person saw three long stalls. The p95 of that list is 200 ms.

For a gesture the bench also reports **settle**, the time for the full-resolution render once the gesture ends, and for each run the tiles computed, the cache hit rate and the peak cache memory. The gate is update p95 at most 16.7 ms.

Three habits keep the numbers honest. Run each scenario on a **copy** of the document with its own cache, so one run does not warm the next. Report **cold** and **warm** separately, because the first render after opening is a different event from the hundredth slider step. And **repeat**: the same cold render of D-01 at 64 px took 332 ms in one run and 317 ms in the next, five percent apart, with nothing changed. A difference smaller than the spread between repeats is not a result.

The web has a standard responsiveness metric, **Interaction to Next Paint** (INP), and its thresholds show how different an editor's needs are. INP calls a page's responsiveness good when its slowest interactions reach the next paint within 200 ms, and poor above 500 ms. Those numbers are for clicks, taps and key presses on pages. A drag across an editing surface is a stream of events, each of which should reach the screen in the next frame, so the bench's gate is a frame, 16.7 ms, a twelfth of INP's good threshold.

## 7.8 Service time is not response time

The bench applies an update, renders, and applies the next only when that render has finished. It measures **service time**: how long the engine takes per update. A person does not wait for the engine. A pointer at 60 Hz produces an event every 16.7 ms whether or not the last one has been drawn, and what the person sees is **response time**: how stale the screen is compared with where the pointer is.

When the engine is faster than the input, the two agree. When it is slower, they do not, and a benchmark that waits for each update before issuing the next hides the difference. This is **coordinated omission**: the benchmark slows down exactly when the engine does, so the slow periods are sampled less than they were lived. Figure 7.2 works through one case.

**Figure 7.2.** Pointer events every 16.7 ms against an engine that needs 45 ms an update and renders the newest event each time. The bench would report 45 ms per update. The screen shows a position between 45 and 102 ms old, 74 ms on average. Computed from the stated rule, not measured.

Two consequences follow. A closed-loop bench like this one is the right tool for comparing engines, since it isolates the engine's cost; it is the wrong tool for saying what a person experiences above the frame budget. And above the budget the screen lags by more than the service time: in Figure 7.2 by 1.6 times as much on average and 2.3 times at worst. To measure response time, issue updates on a fixed clock, as a real pointer does, coalesce as the stage does, and measure from each event's intended time to the frame that shows it or a later event. This is what Gil Tene's wrk2 does for web servers: it sends requests at a constant rate and records each one's latency from the time it should have been sent, after showing that load generators which wait for each response before sending the next miss most of the slow periods they are meant to find. The exercises ask you to add the same to the bench.

## 7.9 Modelled devices

The bench's profiles other than This browser are models: measured time multiplied by a stated factor (1.8 for the older laptop, 3.5 for the tablet, 5 for the phone), with a smaller cache budget that is applied for real. The cache side is therefore measured: evictions, hit rates and recomputation under 16 MB are what this engine does under 16 MB. The time side is a scale, and it assumes every operation slows by the same factor, which real devices do not: memory bandwidth, the JavaScript engine, the GPU and the thermal state each move differently, and a phone that is fast for one second may be slow after thirty. Treat a modelled profile as a question ("would this pass on a device three and a half times slower?") rather than an answer, and calibrate a factor by running the same scenario on the real device and exporting both runs.

## 7.10 Running an experiment

A change to an engine is an experiment, and the bench supports running it as one.

1. **State the prediction with a number.** "Checkpointing before Sharpen should cut the scrub of Vignette from 47 ms to under 30."
2. **Change one thing.** Tile size, eviction policy and preview policy are separate axes in the bench for this reason.
3. **Fix a baseline.** Run the scenarios, then set that run as the baseline in the Bench; later runs show their difference from it.
4. **Repeat** until the spread between repeats is smaller than the effect you are looking for, and report the spread.
5. **Keep the record.** Export the run as CSV or JSON from the Bench's Export menu, with the environment it records, so the result can be compared on another day or another machine.

## Toward a reusable API

```text
request(version, level, tiles, priority)        ask for work; a newer request for the same gesture replaces an older one
priority ∈ input > preview > visible > near > background > persist    the order of Section 7.4
slice(budgetMs) → tiles done                     run jobs until the budget is spent, checking the clock between steps
generation() → n · stale(job) → boolean          cancel by generation, between steps, never inside one
stepTarget(lastArea, lastMs, intervalMs) → area  GIMP's aim: the area that would have filled the interval
yieldToBrowser() → Promise                       scheduler.yield where it exists, else a MessageChannel post
percentiles(list) → { p50, p95, p99 }            latency summaries; the value at ⌊q · n⌋ of the sorted list
openLoop(events every 16.7 ms) → response times  measured from each event's intended time, not its start
```

## Systems in this chapter

| system | unit of work | how big a step is | cancellation |
|---|---|---|---|
| this lab | one tile | fixed tile; 8 ms of tiles per frame | by generation, between tiles; late results kept in the cache |
| GIMP | a chunk of the projection | adapted to fill 1/15 s, median of three aims | stops between chunks when the region changes |
| darktable | one module over a region | the whole region, or tiles when memory is short | atomic flag checked between modules; young runs are left to finish |
| Chromium cc | one tile's raster task | tile size from heuristics | running tasks finish; unstarted ones dropped from the next task graph |
| Krita | a stroke, coarse and full | Instant Preview at a coarse level | the full stroke is computed from the beginning |
| libvips | one region of one sink | a tile shape hinted by the operations | not covered here |
| the browser | one task on the main thread | whatever the code does before returning | `scheduler.yield()` keeps the continuation's priority |

**Table 7.1.** How the systems in this chapter cut work into steps and abandon it.

## Key points

- A frame at 60 Hz is 16.7 ms, and the longest uninterruptible step decides how long input waits, so tile size is also a latency choice.
- The renderer shows a coarse preview at once and refines in 8 ms slices, visible tiles first; on D-01 the preview cut the p95 of a low scrub from 287 ms to 28 ms in one run and to 17 ms in another.
- GIMP adapts its step to fill 1/15 s, which makes fixed costs vanish but overshoots after a cost change by the ratio of new cost to old, twice because of its median; a fixed tile never overshoots by more than one tile.
- Rank work by urgency, coalesce input to the newest event, and cancel by generation; with content keys, late results still fill the cache.
- darktable lets a run that started less than half its average time ago finish and restarts after it, which coalesces changes; Chromium never cancels a running raster task.
- Workers take the work off the main thread, transfers move tiles without copying, and shared memory needs cross-origin isolation, which also buys a 5 µs clock instead of 100 µs.
- Measure latency as percentiles, repeat until the spread is smaller than the effect, and hold an editor to a frame, not to INP's 200 ms.
- A closed-loop bench measures service time; to measure what a person sees, issue events at a constant rate and time each from when it should have been sent, as wrk2 does.
- Modelled profiles scale time and apply a real cache budget, so calibrate the factor on a real device before trusting it.
